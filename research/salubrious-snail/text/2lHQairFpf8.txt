[0:00] The other day I was exploring options for a&nbsp; go-wide beatdown deck, so naturally I was on&nbsp;&nbsp; the EDHRec pages of Toski and Tovolar. With&nbsp; a commander that’s low-mana enough not to be&nbsp;&nbsp; boosted out by the ramp part and who draws enough&nbsp; cards to not need the tacked on land-to-hand.&nbsp;&nbsp; You could spend that three mana on literally&nbsp; anything else. There’s also some questionable&nbsp;&nbsp; draw run in these decks whose commander is an&nbsp; insanely powerful draw spell. So, what gives?

[0:46] Well, I think this ties into a culture of hailing&nbsp; certain cards as must-plays, one that exists both&nbsp;&nbsp; online and in IRL circles. This sort of effect&nbsp; has likely always been somewhat the case among&nbsp;&nbsp; various circles in Magic: the Gathering, since&nbsp; it’s a social game with an established community,&nbsp;&nbsp; but EDH is especially vulnerable to cycles&nbsp; of over-hype about particular cards. This&nbsp;&nbsp; is because it’s an even more social format&nbsp; than the baseline due to having four players,&nbsp;&nbsp; and also, the fact that it’s singleton and&nbsp; 100 cards means that there’s kind of a lot&nbsp;&nbsp; of room for fluff in decks and it’ll take&nbsp; longer for players to identify a mediocre&nbsp;&nbsp; card than it would in a 60 card format. There&nbsp; will be the cards that truly should be added&nbsp;&nbsp; to most decks on the basis of power—Sol Ring,&nbsp; Counterspell, Mystic Remora—and you can go check&nbsp;&nbsp; out my budget cards video if you want my saucy,&nbsp; saucy opinions about them.

[1:31] But there will also&nbsp;&nbsp; be cards regarded in the same lens but which&nbsp; truly don’t belong in all or even most decks,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I want to focus on a specific category&nbsp; of cards among these: mana ramp spells. First, a quick overview. Ramp&nbsp; makes your amount of mana go up,&nbsp;&nbsp; typically permanently barring interaction&nbsp; from your opponents. It’s nigh-ubiquitous&nbsp;&nbsp; in EDH due to the slow speed of the&nbsp; format, with the first and second&nbsp;&nbsp; most played nonland cards in the format both&nbsp; being ramp spells. And don’t get me wrong,&nbsp;&nbsp; ramp is fantastic and there are lots of great&nbsp; ramp spells out there. But as you can probably&nbsp;&nbsp; guess by the first couple minutes of this video,&nbsp; I have opinions about some of them. Specifically,&nbsp;&nbsp; I think people gravitate toward certain ones&nbsp; that are considered “staples”, even when there&nbsp;&nbsp; are other options that are much better suited to&nbsp; their deck.

[2:19] So, treat this video as a guide—how&nbsp;&nbsp; to stop jamming Cultivate into every deck, and&nbsp; figure out what your deck actually wants instead.&nbsp;&nbsp; Hopefully there’s plenty of nuance to be gleaned&nbsp; here, but this is a video principally stemming&nbsp;&nbsp; from me getting upsetti spaghetti about&nbsp; Cultivate, so let’s talk about that first. Cultivate, along with its twin Kodama’s Reach, is&nbsp; a Rampant Growth that costs an extra mana and has&nbsp;&nbsp; a Lay of the Land attached. It’s a straightforward&nbsp; formula with some clear appeal in a slow format,&nbsp;&nbsp; and evidently Wizards agreed in 2011—it’s&nbsp; in all three green precons in the first&nbsp;&nbsp; EDH set. This alone likely contributed&nbsp; to the card’s popularity, and honestly,&nbsp;&nbsp; the card is right at home in these decks—three&nbsp; color decks with questionable manabases and a&nbsp;&nbsp; healthy spattering of 7-9 mana cards.

[3:05] When you&nbsp; play Cultivate, you’re actively choosing an extra&nbsp;&nbsp; land to hand rather than the speed of a two mana&nbsp; ramp spell, and this is absolutely a tradeoff that&nbsp;&nbsp; some decks want. The more colors you play, the&nbsp; more that extra land will matter, and colorfixing&nbsp;&nbsp; might be a valuable goal in its own right if&nbsp; you’re running a lot of colors with not a lot&nbsp;&nbsp; of budget. If you’re playing five color and you&nbsp; have any three colors that include green, a turn&nbsp;&nbsp; three Cultivate sets you up for a turn 4 with a&nbsp; mana of each color available—that’s a great deal! This usefulness as a setup piece is most obvious&nbsp; in a five color deck, but the same logic also&nbsp;&nbsp; works in a deck with fewer colors—as a general&nbsp; principle Cultivate is best geared toward decks&nbsp;&nbsp; looking for a pivotal 5 or 6 mana turn.

[3:45] This&nbsp; brings me to a major consideration when picking&nbsp;&nbsp; out ramp—mana curve. A lot of decks will have one&nbsp; or more major mana value numbers they care about,&nbsp;&nbsp; and your ramp package should be formulated to&nbsp; fit this. My Glissa deck wants to play Glissa&nbsp;&nbsp; on turn 2 whenever possible followed up with&nbsp; more ramp spells, so if we look at a graph of&nbsp;&nbsp; the mana value of the deck’s ramp spells, it’s&nbsp; big spikes on 1 and 4 mana, and a smaller spike&nbsp;&nbsp; on 7 because the deck is a disgusting greed&nbsp; pile. There are only a few two and three cost&nbsp;&nbsp; ramp spells since the deck prefers to skip 2&nbsp; mana and play Glissa on 3 mana, and the ramp&nbsp;&nbsp; spells of these costs that are in the deck either&nbsp; create untapped lands or have flexible mana costs.&nbsp;&nbsp; When you’re adding a ramp spell to a deck, you&nbsp; should be asking “what is this ramping toward?”&nbsp;&nbsp; Wanting more lands in general isn’t necessarily&nbsp; the wrong answer, but if the most important&nbsp;&nbsp; mana value you’re pushing toward is four, you&nbsp; shouldn’t run a bunch of three mana ramp spells.

[4:43] This is part of what prompts my eyebrow raising&nbsp; when I see 60% of Toski decks running the card.&nbsp;&nbsp; It doesn’t ramp into the commander, it takes up a&nbsp; mana slot that could have gone to a card-drawing&nbsp;&nbsp; beater, and the commander itself encourages volume&nbsp; of creatures rather than size. This means you&nbsp;&nbsp; probably don’t want that many cards on 5 or 6 mana&nbsp; other than, I dunno, Mr. Squirrels. The commander&nbsp;&nbsp; also draws you a ton of cards, and if your deck&nbsp; has 33 forests, each of those draws has a healthy&nbsp;&nbsp; one third chance of getting you the exact land&nbsp; you want. You’re playing a mono-colored deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; and if you play Cultivate you’re effectively&nbsp; overpaying for ramp that gives you an upside&nbsp;&nbsp; you don’t need. I find Cultivate questionable in&nbsp; Tovolar for similar reasons—two colors is still&nbsp;&nbsp; few enough that the color fixing probably isn’t&nbsp; necessary, and werewolves top end consists of,&nbsp;&nbsp; like, a handful of 5 and 6 drops?

[5:30] Rampant&nbsp; Growth is almost certainly better but I’m&nbsp;&nbsp; not sure I’d even go for that over a two&nbsp; drop beater that can draw you some cards. Card type is another consideration with regard&nbsp; to ramp, since artifacts and especially creatures&nbsp;&nbsp; will die to removal and board wipes, but land&nbsp; destruction is much less common. However,&nbsp;&nbsp; dorks and rocks are often more efficiently&nbsp; costed than cards that grab lands,&nbsp;&nbsp; so there are tradeoffs to be had. They all&nbsp; have their place, but the places tend to be&nbsp;&nbsp; different. Obviously, for non-green decks, bless&nbsp; their hearts, you’re mostly stuck with rocks,&nbsp;&nbsp; but that doesn’t necessarily mean green decks&nbsp; will never want rocks. Single green dorks tend&nbsp;&nbsp; to be the most mana-efficient ramp option&nbsp; at lower budgets, so I tend to go for them&nbsp;&nbsp; in any deck where the 3 cmc slot is important&nbsp; but the 2 cmc slot is unimportant.

[6:13] My Glissa&nbsp;&nbsp; deck and my Meria deck both have three mana&nbsp; commanders that they try to land on curve,&nbsp;&nbsp; but Glissa runs dorks because it has very few&nbsp; 2 mana plays and Meria runs rocks because the 1&nbsp;&nbsp; and 2 mana slots are very important for the deck.&nbsp; In a third one of my green decks, Scapeshift is&nbsp;&nbsp; a key piece of the deck, so the deck runs mostly&nbsp; land-grabbing spells in its ramp suite even though&nbsp;&nbsp; they’re slower than single green dorks. Long story&nbsp; short, different decks will want different things,&nbsp;&nbsp; and these are just a handful of examples&nbsp; of what I ended up on with my green decks. A final consideration when considering ramp&nbsp; cards is the net mana cost vs the upfront&nbsp;&nbsp; mana cost.

[6:54] For something like Rampant Growth or&nbsp; Cultivate, these values are identical since they&nbsp;&nbsp; don’t immediately offer more mana, but some&nbsp; spells give you untapped lands. For example,&nbsp;&nbsp; Skyshroud Claim has an upfront cost of 4, but&nbsp; a net cost of 2 if you grab Forests that enter&nbsp;&nbsp; untapped. Two ramp spells I generally prefer&nbsp; to Cultivate in 2 color decks are Harrow and&nbsp;&nbsp; Grow from the Ashes, as they grab untapped lands&nbsp; and thereby allow for more flexible turns than a&nbsp;&nbsp; card that grabs tapped lands. If we look at mana&nbsp; rocks, most theoretically offer mana immediately,&nbsp;&nbsp; but in practice that can differ by when they’re&nbsp; played and what deck is playing them. Signets can&nbsp;&nbsp; be a net mana cost of 1 after turn 2 if the&nbsp; symbols line up, but on 2 mana they can’t do&nbsp;&nbsp; much else.

[7:36] Similarly, colorless-tapping rocks&nbsp; like Mind Stone add mana immediately if you&nbsp;&nbsp; have a place to put one colorless mana, but if&nbsp; you don’t they’re just the sticker mana cost. I like to think I’ve offered a little toolbox&nbsp; here in picking out the most appropriate ramp,&nbsp;&nbsp; and as a final point I’ll address an important&nbsp; question that I’ve been skirting around so&nbsp;&nbsp; far—that being how much ramp a deck should&nbsp; run. This question honestly depends on so&nbsp;&nbsp; many factors that I’d feel irresponsible offering&nbsp; a straightforward answer or even a framework. The&nbsp;&nbsp; best way to tell the right amount of your deck&nbsp; is, well, to play it and see if it feels like it&nbsp;&nbsp; has the right amount of ramp. If you’re ramping&nbsp; to a particular value, does the deck succeed at&nbsp;&nbsp; that consistently? [8:21] There are also differences&nbsp; in the size of mana ramp spell, so if you find&nbsp;&nbsp; yourself drawing too many ramp spells but want&nbsp; the same ramping capability, you can cut some&nbsp;&nbsp; small ones for a bigger one or two. Conversely,&nbsp; you can do the opposite maneuver if your opening&nbsp;&nbsp; hands seem consistently loaded with expensive&nbsp; ramp spells you can’t get to quickly enough. There may very well be strong ramp spells—Elvish&nbsp; Mystic, Nature’s Lore, Thought Vessel—that don’t&nbsp;&nbsp; end up being quite what your deck wants, and this&nbsp; is okay. The best ramp solution isn’t gonna be&nbsp;&nbsp; one size fits all. At the end of the day, what I’m&nbsp; trying to say here is simply that you should look&nbsp;&nbsp; at your deck’s ramp suite critically. I think when&nbsp; it comes to the traditionally simpler parts of an&nbsp;&nbsp; EDH deck—the lands, the removal, the ramp—a lot&nbsp; of people have the idea that they can kinda gloss&nbsp;&nbsp; over these things and focus their efforts on the&nbsp; juicy synergies that comprise the spiritual core&nbsp;&nbsp; of the deck.

[9:14] But these parts are what lets&nbsp; the core of the deck do its thing smoothly,&nbsp;&nbsp; so taking a critical eye toward them will&nbsp; likely improve your deck more than you think.
