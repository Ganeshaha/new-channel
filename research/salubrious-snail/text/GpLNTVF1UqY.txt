[0:00] Conventionally speaking, aggro is a fairly&nbsp; straightforward archetype. Your opponent is at&nbsp;&nbsp; a life total. The rules of the game say you need&nbsp; to turn that life total to zero. You try to do so,&nbsp;&nbsp; as quickly as possible. Common ways of doing&nbsp; this consist of direct damage, small creatures,&nbsp;&nbsp; large creatures rushed out quickly, and so&nbsp; forth. Red Deck Wins is instructive here:&nbsp;&nbsp; a mixture of burn spells and small creatures&nbsp; means that the aggro player will try to deal&nbsp;&nbsp; as much creature damage as possible early&nbsp; and then hope that they can run the deck&nbsp;&nbsp; to the finish line with burn. [0:46] Or 13 swings with an elite squad of&nbsp;&nbsp; three Delvers of Secret. Hmmm. Perplexing. The&nbsp; answer is that it doesn’t work. The fact that&nbsp;&nbsp; EDH is a 40 life format is a pain point for our&nbsp; hypothetical red-deck-winner. With this in mind,&nbsp;&nbsp; I think an instructive exercise is to&nbsp; think about what that 40 life really means. First of all, lifepay is so much less punishing.&nbsp; Fetches and shocks? Easy. Phyrexian mana symbols?&nbsp;&nbsp; Close enough to free. Paying 1 life for&nbsp; 1 card? An even more delicious bargain&nbsp;&nbsp; than usual. [1:28] Secondly, blocking&nbsp; isn’t as necessary. Decks are empowered to&nbsp;&nbsp; constantly use tap abilities, to swing in&nbsp; for value with a Bident of Thassa effect,&nbsp;&nbsp; and to eat 5-10 damage hits rather than blocking&nbsp; with an important creature. They’re also empowered&nbsp;&nbsp; to spend their early turns throwing down snowbally&nbsp; value cards rather than committing much to board. So, with that stuff in mind—what happens if&nbsp; you remove that extra health and take away&nbsp;&nbsp; those things? Suddenly that Phyrexian Arena is a&nbsp; liability. That Dismember is close to regaining&nbsp;&nbsp; its status as a three mana black card. Your&nbsp; commander might need to be a blocker if you&nbsp;&nbsp; didn’t add enough creatures to your deck, and&nbsp; trading is the best case scenario. Spending a turn&nbsp;&nbsp; playing setup cards for an approaching big turn&nbsp; goes from an exciting proposition to a terrifying&nbsp;&nbsp; one when you might be the one to get knocked out&nbsp; if you skimp on blockers.

[2:17] Your hands are tied,&nbsp;&nbsp; your knees are quivering, and your deck&nbsp; decidedly was not built for this situation. To me, this is the best starting point for looking&nbsp; at EDH aggro. Your goal doesn’t start and end&nbsp;&nbsp; with reducing your opponent’s life total to zero&nbsp; like it generally does in 20 health 1v1 formats,&nbsp;&nbsp; because cutting out a large chunk of an&nbsp; opponent’s life total is helpful in its&nbsp;&nbsp; own right. My mantra in a game where somebody&nbsp; has a deck that is concerning and unpredictable&nbsp;&nbsp; but not necessarily totally dominant, is that they&nbsp; should be at 15 life. When somebody is at 15 life,&nbsp;&nbsp; they have to play much more conservatively.&nbsp; Having safe setup turns isn’t a given,&nbsp;&nbsp; and they’re generally one round of attacks away&nbsp; from death if they start doing concerning things.

[3:01] Going forward, I’m going to refer to this concept&nbsp; as Aggro Ideology. This is not the beginning and&nbsp;&nbsp; end of what aggro means in EDH, but simply&nbsp; a phrase I use to gesture at the benefits of&nbsp;&nbsp; having opponents who are scared about their life&nbsp; total. If I had to pick out a second feature of&nbsp;&nbsp; the commander format that’s important for aggro&nbsp; decks, it’s commander damage. If your commander&nbsp;&nbsp; deals 21 combat damage to an opponent, they lose.&nbsp; This means that if your deck is commander based,&nbsp;&nbsp; you’re playing a 21 health format instead of the&nbsp; much more challenging 40 health format. Neat! [4:27] The last type of deck here is disruptive&nbsp;&nbsp; aggro. This is a deck that fully commits to the&nbsp; plan of making its opponents uncomfortable. You’ll&nbsp;&nbsp; commonly see lots of cards that damage multiple&nbsp; opponents, often paired with some form of stax.&nbsp;&nbsp; I’ve included links to examples to each of&nbsp; these types of decks in the description. Now that I’ve laid out a conceptual foundation&nbsp; for aggro, let’s talk about the specific goals&nbsp;&nbsp; and challenges that are important within&nbsp; a game. For a creature-based aggro deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; the basic idea is simple: you want creatures&nbsp; on board, and you want them to connect with&nbsp;&nbsp; your opponents’ life totals. To connect with life&nbsp; totals, you need to get past blockers. The most&nbsp;&nbsp; common way of going about this is evasion, either&nbsp; innately on the aggressive creatures or applied&nbsp;&nbsp; through equipments or cards such as Wonder.

[5:10] An&nbsp; alternative method is eliminating those blockers,&nbsp;&nbsp; but this is much less common—one example is&nbsp; a budget Rakdos discard deck I built that&nbsp;&nbsp; runs Malfegor as the commander mostly&nbsp; as a way of clearing out blockers. Maintaining creatures on board is a separate&nbsp; challenge, with the roadblock there usually being&nbsp;&nbsp; boardwipes. There’s no one foolproof solution to&nbsp; this, but here are some potential workarounds.&nbsp; For one, be faster than the deck&nbsp; with the boardwipes, and target&nbsp;&nbsp; them first. This is likely the preferred&nbsp; method for higher power voltron decks.&nbsp; Alternatively run protection&nbsp; spells—permanent-based is preferable&nbsp;&nbsp; for an aggro deck, with Selfless Spirit&nbsp; being the absolute gold standard if you’re&nbsp;&nbsp; running white.

[5:51] You can also run instants that&nbsp; protect your creatures, or even counter-spells,&nbsp;&nbsp; but keep in mind that holding up mana will make&nbsp; your deck less mana efficient unless you have&nbsp;&nbsp; treasures or unless your creatures have flash. As a third option, run Blood Artist effects—this&nbsp;&nbsp; is my preferred strategy when playing black, as&nbsp; it ties in beautifully with our Aggro Ideology. The point about outrunning boardwipes ties&nbsp; into a broader point about the speed of a&nbsp;&nbsp; deck. Speed and resilience are factors&nbsp; that need to be considered—a deck that&nbsp;&nbsp; can outrun most combos and kill midrange&nbsp; before it stabilizes might not need much&nbsp;&nbsp; in the way of counterplays or resilience&nbsp; to obstacles, but a slower deck might want&nbsp;&nbsp; a lot more of those things. Past the basics, a key skill you’ll want&nbsp; to develop is target prioritization. If&nbsp;&nbsp; your deck prefers to kill one player at a&nbsp; time, you need to pick a player to focus,&nbsp;&nbsp; and picking wrong might lose you the game.&nbsp; Primary targets that come to mind are players&nbsp;&nbsp; who pay a lot of life, and players who have&nbsp; especially strong ways to shut you down,&nbsp;&nbsp; but it’s definitely a vibes-based thing and when&nbsp; in doubt you can just attack the opponent you’re&nbsp;&nbsp; most afraid of.
