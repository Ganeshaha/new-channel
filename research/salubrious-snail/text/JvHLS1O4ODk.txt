[0:00] A few months ago, I had a brilliant&nbsp; idea: I’d make a tribal gates deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; with Child of Alara as the commander. I’d run&nbsp; 55 lands along with a bunch of draw and ramp,&nbsp;&nbsp; with Maze’s End, Crackling Perimeter, and a&nbsp; handful of beatdown options being my wincons.&nbsp;&nbsp; A plethora of ways to sacrifice Child of Alara&nbsp; means that I should have board control mostly&nbsp;&nbsp; handled, so I only need a mere five slots of&nbsp; the deck dedicated to interaction of all types.&nbsp;&nbsp; How efficient. Finally, a few options for big&nbsp; lifegain means that once my land count is up&nbsp;&nbsp; where I want it, I can heal up the damage it took&nbsp; me to get there. It’s not a high-caliber deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; but it certainly scratched my goofy-deck itch,&nbsp; and it seemed pretty solid for what it was.&nbsp;&nbsp; Unfortunately it was a terrible idea,&nbsp; chiefly because of the commander.

[0:46] Now, allow me to lay my credentials on&nbsp; the table. I am a cruel control player&nbsp;&nbsp; at heart. My first commander deck was mono black&nbsp; ruin-your-day with Sheoldred as the commander.&nbsp;&nbsp; My first deck I played with at sanctioned&nbsp; events was a standard Grixis control deck&nbsp;&nbsp; I built just after the release of Born of the&nbsp; Gods. And my favorite draft I ever played in&nbsp;&nbsp; was a Dragons of Tarkir draft where I picked up&nbsp; five flattens and seven other removal spells,&nbsp;&nbsp; and spent my night gleefully watching the joy&nbsp; drain from my opponents’ eyes as creature after&nbsp;&nbsp; creature bit the dust immediately after it was&nbsp; played. So when I tell you I want a commander&nbsp;&nbsp; that just repetitively boardwipes to be a good&nbsp; idea, I mean it. But it’s not a good idea.

[1:31] In the context of commander, boardwipes are a&nbsp; type of effect that I would call “asymmetric”.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is confusing on the surface, since they’re&nbsp; generally literally symmetrical effects—you&nbsp;&nbsp; know, they destroy everything. But what&nbsp; I mean here is that they impact different&nbsp;&nbsp; players massively differently. Somebody playing a&nbsp; low-mid power synergistic midrange deck will feel&nbsp;&nbsp; them very brutally, but there are a lot of types&nbsp; of decks that just won’t really care as much.&nbsp;&nbsp; Board wipes won’t stop combos, they won’t&nbsp; prevent aggro decks from abusing your life total,&nbsp;&nbsp; they won’t slow down large land counts, and they&nbsp; won’t shut down your opponents generating a lot&nbsp;&nbsp; of instant and sorcery-based value. The best&nbsp; option my Child of Alara deck has for dealing&nbsp;&nbsp; with somebody suddenly winning is using one of the&nbsp; two counterspells within the deck, or preparing&nbsp;&nbsp; to instant-speed sacrifice Child of Alara and&nbsp; hoping I don’t encounter interaction of any kind.

[2:26] Okay. So, the Child of Alara dependent game plan&nbsp; has some holes. Big whoop, I’m playing a gates&nbsp;&nbsp; deck man, no shit my deck has weaknesses. However,&nbsp; this is a bigger problem than it seems. Going back&nbsp;&nbsp; to the idea of the card being asymmetric, we can&nbsp; think about how it is experienced by the decks&nbsp;&nbsp; that are more vulnerable to it. And the answer&nbsp; is it’s hell. It sucks so hard for these decks,&nbsp;&nbsp; they can barely even start to build a board&nbsp; before they’re staring down the barrel of&nbsp;&nbsp; another Child of Alara sac. Even if they deal with&nbsp; one of my wipes, as long as my ramp game worked&nbsp;&nbsp; out as intended there will just be another one&nbsp; immediately following it. This is similar to the&nbsp;&nbsp; graveyard decks analogy from my last video—this&nbsp; deck will ruin the day of casual synergy decks,&nbsp;&nbsp; but against a less board-dependent deck it’ll&nbsp; putter around and be a mild nuisance before&nbsp;&nbsp; ultimately getting crushed, with little to no&nbsp; chance of actually pulling off one of its wincons.

[3:21] Now, Child of Alara is the most extreme&nbsp; example of board wipes creating highly&nbsp;&nbsp; asymmetric game experiences, but this&nbsp; is an issue with board wipes as a whole.&nbsp;&nbsp; I talked a bit about my Glissa deck in my midrange&nbsp; video, and that deck operates as a kind of homage&nbsp;&nbsp; to the mono black Sheoldred deck I played when&nbsp; I was 15. Get card advantage and a ton of mana,&nbsp;&nbsp; and proceed to throw bombs and one-sided board&nbsp; wipes at the table until my opponents fold.&nbsp;&nbsp; Now, that deck is much more well-rounded than&nbsp; the theoretical Child of Alara deck. It has&nbsp;&nbsp; a lot of single target removal to deal with&nbsp; key threats, and a variety of wincons which&nbsp;&nbsp; can all eliminate a threatening player much&nbsp; faster than Maze’s End or Crackling Perimeter. Nonetheless, the profile of how it fares&nbsp; against different decks looks very similar.&nbsp; I try not to pull out the deck against the low-mid&nbsp; power midrange decks that make up a decent portion&nbsp;&nbsp; of decks in my playgroup, because it generally&nbsp; just stomps on these decks in theatrical fashion.&nbsp;&nbsp; However, when my opponents have pressure&nbsp; to prevent me from fully greeding out or&nbsp;&nbsp; counterspells for my massive mana investments or&nbsp; combo wins to catch me and my mostly-sorcery-speed&nbsp;&nbsp;

[4:26] removal suite off guard, it’s much&nbsp; worse. Against these sorts of plays,&nbsp;&nbsp; my deck ends up looking like one of the&nbsp; clunky midrange decks that I usually crush. Before going further, I should take the&nbsp; opportunity to state that board wipes aren’t bad&nbsp;&nbsp; overall. A lot of decks are heavily board-based,&nbsp; and it’s good to have a solid way to deal with&nbsp;&nbsp; that. A better version of my Glissa deck would&nbsp; probably be running 3-5 board wipes instead of 8,&nbsp;&nbsp; with tutors to bridge the gap, but years spent as&nbsp; a mono-black player forced me into the hard truth&nbsp;&nbsp; that unconditional tutors are bland as hell, so&nbsp; I don’t play them. Ultimately, the deck is what&nbsp;&nbsp; I want it to be, and I’ll happily let it serve&nbsp; as a teaching point as happily as I’ll play it.&nbsp;&nbsp; The real thing I’m trying to convey here is the&nbsp; need to be mindful when constructing your deck.&nbsp;&nbsp; The interaction package of your deck, and what&nbsp; things it’s particularly good or bad against,&nbsp;&nbsp; is something you should think about.&nbsp; Ideally, it should be well-balanced,&nbsp;&nbsp; and if there are holes they should be&nbsp; intentional rather than incidental.

[5:24] As an example, my Meria equipments deck has&nbsp; very little hard removal—burn and noncreature&nbsp;&nbsp; permanent destruction, yes, but not much for&nbsp; big creatures. This is mostly a matter of the&nbsp;&nbsp; fact that I’m playing an aggro deck, and I’m&nbsp; looking to get a substantial board presence&nbsp;&nbsp; early and be threatening life totals. The deck’s&nbsp; removal serves to slow down my opponents and&nbsp;&nbsp; deal with pesky cards like Trigon Predator,&nbsp; but ultimately the deck’s solution to later&nbsp;&nbsp; threats is to simply deal with the player,&nbsp; or politic around the threat for a while.&nbsp;&nbsp; My deck’s removal suite has a hole, but it’s&nbsp; a calculated one. Another common hole in an&nbsp;&nbsp; interaction suite would be fast blue decks&nbsp; that choose to almost entirely run counters&nbsp;&nbsp; that don’t target creatures—a choice motivated by&nbsp; the fact that most forms of interaction from their&nbsp;&nbsp; opponents are noncreature, and an essence scatter&nbsp; isn’t gonna help protect a turn 4 combo win.

[6:13] As a final note, let’s return to the idea of&nbsp; asymmetric effects. A lot of stax effects and&nbsp;&nbsp; hate cards also fall under this label, but stax&nbsp; effects generally operate the opposite way as&nbsp;&nbsp; board wipes—choking out burstier strategies&nbsp; that carefully plan out their resources to&nbsp;&nbsp; execute a specific gameplan, while having less&nbsp; of an effect on a deck whose gameplan consists&nbsp;&nbsp; of “play treefolk” or something. There’s a reason&nbsp; why running a load of stax effects is a better&nbsp;&nbsp; idea than running a load of board wipes—the sorts&nbsp; of decks shut down by an Archon of Emeria are&nbsp;&nbsp; generally stronger and more carefully constructed&nbsp; than the sort of decks shut down by a Plague Wind.&nbsp;&nbsp; You can build a deck with a bunch of stax effects&nbsp; and a win plan that can outrun a midrange deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; but you can’t build a deck full of boardwipes&nbsp; with a win plan that can outrun a combo deck.&nbsp;&nbsp; And if you build a deck that massacres casual&nbsp; midrange decks but flails around against&nbsp;&nbsp; everything else, you’re going to be struggling&nbsp; to create interesting and dynamic games.
