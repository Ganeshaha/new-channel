[0:00] This is a video about a mechanic I love, a&nbsp; commander card I hate, and a bizarre deck&nbsp;&nbsp; I built out of spite. Our tale begins in 1998,&nbsp; with Urza’s Saga bringing all sorts of horrendous&nbsp;&nbsp; bullshit to the world, and also a gift—the cycling&nbsp; mechanic. It’s a beautifully simple mechanic: you&nbsp;&nbsp; can pay an alternate cost on a card and discard&nbsp; it in order to draw a card. This began in simple&nbsp;&nbsp; fashion during Urza Block, but five years later&nbsp; Onslaught block added a ton of dimension to the&nbsp;&nbsp; mechanic. Varied cycle costs, triggers attached&nbsp; to cycle abilities, cycling for basic land types,&nbsp;&nbsp; and six cards with triggered abilities when&nbsp; other cards were cycled. These six were varying&nbsp;&nbsp; power levels, with one in particular being just…&nbsp; excellent, but we’ll talk about that a bit later.

[0:44] Cycling has seen additional prints every&nbsp; few years since, and for a simple reason:&nbsp;&nbsp; people like this mechanic. As an alternate cost&nbsp; ability it adds a lot of flexibility to cards,&nbsp;&nbsp; which leads to interesting decisionmaking. Every&nbsp; cycling card a player draws prompts the question&nbsp;&nbsp; “is this card better than a different card plus&nbsp; a small mana commitment?” This is nice in aggro&nbsp;&nbsp; when running out of gas might be a concern,&nbsp; and it’s even more useful in a control deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; where that mana cost can be spent on the end of an&nbsp; opponent’s turn, or even in response to things to&nbsp;&nbsp; dig for answers. On top of this, since cycled&nbsp; cards are discarded and go to the graveyard,&nbsp;&nbsp; they work well with graveyard synergies, providing&nbsp; a near effortless way to put cards in the yard.&nbsp;&nbsp; And that’s only the playstyles helped out&nbsp; by the mechanic innately. Remember that&nbsp;&nbsp; cool card from Onslaught I mentioned?

[1:25] Astral&nbsp; Slide has seen a ton of experimentation ever&nbsp;&nbsp; since it was printed, as it synergizes with many&nbsp; different cards and offers a ton of flexibility.&nbsp;&nbsp; As a result it was kind of understood as&nbsp; the core of any cycling deck before 2020. Astral Slide decks have a super interesting&nbsp; playstyle that revolves heavily around strategic&nbsp;&nbsp; resource management, and in a way that feels&nbsp; very in-line with the fundamental nature of&nbsp;&nbsp; the mechanic. Number of cycling cards in hand&nbsp; constantly dictates the strategic direction of&nbsp;&nbsp; the deck—when you have more, you can get&nbsp; bolder and set up for more daring turns,&nbsp;&nbsp; as well as using the slide triggers more&nbsp; liberally—as blocker elimination, to net&nbsp;&nbsp; small value off of ETBs, and so forth, or casting&nbsp; a cycling card normally. By contrast, when you&nbsp;&nbsp; have fewer cycling cards in hand, each one is pure&nbsp; gold.

[2:06] You always want to save them for protecting&nbsp;&nbsp; a critical creature or disrupting a dangerous&nbsp; play from an opponent, but if you feel the game&nbsp;&nbsp; slipping away you can also choose to gamble that&nbsp; lifeline on an endstep for a little bit of value. In addition, mana is a super important resource&nbsp; for both Astral Slide and the mechanic as a whole,&nbsp;&nbsp; as land counts also dictate how safe you feel&nbsp; playing setup pieces, and how bursty your turns&nbsp;&nbsp; can be. A more controlling cycling deck constantly&nbsp; forces a player to think probabilistically—if I&nbsp;&nbsp; spend mana to play a spell on my turn, what are&nbsp; the odds I’ll be able to handle a key threat on&nbsp;&nbsp; my opponent’s turn if it gets played? What are&nbsp; my odds of drawing an additional cycling card&nbsp;&nbsp; or an answer off the top?

[2:47] How much mana would I&nbsp; need to have the best chance of averting death&nbsp;&nbsp; in a worst case scenario? These questions are&nbsp; also fundamentally tied to deck construction,&nbsp;&nbsp; since a player must always weigh the strength of a&nbsp; cycling card vs the cycling cost of it. As a card,&nbsp;&nbsp; Akroma’s Vengeance is wildly more powerful&nbsp; than Winged Shepherd, but if I’m sitting&nbsp;&nbsp; there on an opponent’s turn with four mana&nbsp; open, looking down the barrel of defeat,&nbsp;&nbsp; I know which of the two I’d rather have in&nbsp; hand. In short, the cycling mechanic creates and&nbsp;&nbsp; promotes far more textured and strategic gameplay&nbsp; than the vast majority of mechanics in MTG. With all of that deep strategic texture in mind,&nbsp; let’s talk about Gavi, Nest Warden. It’s a 5 mana&nbsp;&nbsp; 2/5 who makes the first cycle each turn free,&nbsp; and who creates a cat when you draw your second&nbsp;&nbsp; card each turn.

[3:33] This card is strong enough to be&nbsp; a natural choice for a cycling commander, and also&nbsp;&nbsp; deathly boring. Free cycles on each turn removes&nbsp; an entire dimension of the cycling mechanic,&nbsp;&nbsp; and on top of that it highly encourages a&nbsp; stable quantity of cycling—one per turn,&nbsp;&nbsp; maybe two on an opponent’s turn sometimes. Every&nbsp; cycle past that is a waste, since you have to pay&nbsp;&nbsp; for it and you don’t even get a cute little&nbsp; dino-cat, and every turn you fail to cycle is&nbsp;&nbsp; also a waste. C’mon, it’s free real estate. This&nbsp; ability and the associated incentives remove the&nbsp;&nbsp; status of cycling cards as an important&nbsp; resource to be saved and used carefully,&nbsp;&nbsp; and also in turn leave players with a lot of&nbsp; extra mana they didn’t have to plan out. What&nbsp;&nbsp; does that mana get spent on?

[4:13] Well, permanents that&nbsp; generate value when cards get cycled, of course. Gavi destroys most of the cool, strategic&nbsp; decision-making generally involved with&nbsp;&nbsp; playing a cycling deck, and replaces it with&nbsp; yet another do-the-thing value-pile midrange&nbsp;&nbsp; deck. The design of most Gavi decks is such that&nbsp; if you do the thing—cycling in this case—you get&nbsp;&nbsp; an effortless mixture of card advantage&nbsp; and board advantage spread out over time,&nbsp;&nbsp; just like the other 80% of EDH decks that exist&nbsp; out there. And this isn’t because Gavi players&nbsp;&nbsp; are people who like especially boring decks—the&nbsp; card is precision designed to encourage exactly&nbsp;&nbsp; this type of gameplay, even if you build from&nbsp; scratch rather than buying the precon. It’s&nbsp;&nbsp; a betrayal on the part of Wizards of the Coast&nbsp; of everything cool about the cycling mechanic,&nbsp;&nbsp; and it creates totally predictable&nbsp; gameplay—well, except for one thing.

[5:04] You see, the other thing that Gavi incentivizes&nbsp; is cards with high cycling costs. This is&nbsp;&nbsp; maybe the one thing about the card that’s not&nbsp; dead-boring, but not really in a good way? Okay,&nbsp;&nbsp; so this incentivization is most relevant for&nbsp; one specific cycle of cards from Scourge,&nbsp;&nbsp; and since Gavi doesn’t pay the&nbsp; bonus cost of Decree of Justice,&nbsp;&nbsp; it’s actually mainly relevant for two cards.&nbsp; The first is Decree of Silence. Big whoop,&nbsp;&nbsp; it’s a free counterspell you’ll have sometimes.&nbsp; Not much to say here. The other is Decree of&nbsp;&nbsp; Annihilation. I’ll here deliver a hot take—nuking&nbsp; lands is maybe okay, maybe pretty cool,&nbsp;&nbsp; if your playgroup is down for that, and also if&nbsp; you actually have a way of ending the game pretty&nbsp;&nbsp; soon after using that wild swing of momentum.&nbsp; But nothing else about Gavi really promotes&nbsp;&nbsp; that sort of deck-style.

[5:53] More likely, you’ll&nbsp; keep slowly getting cats, everybody’s manabase&nbsp;&nbsp; will gradually recover, and congratulations:&nbsp; you’ve just doubled the length of this game.&nbsp;&nbsp; Gavi into Decree of Annihilation, while certainly&nbsp; unusual, still kinda fails to be interesting. So, we have here this pretty bland commander,&nbsp; and if you ask an EDH player what they think&nbsp;&nbsp; about cycling, she’s what they think of. This cool&nbsp; mechanic with a rich history and tons of synergy&nbsp;&nbsp; potential, reduced down to yet another value pile&nbsp; commander. Now, to any Gavi players out there,&nbsp;&nbsp; I want to say: this is not an attack on you, and&nbsp; Gavi is more interesting than a lot of commanders&nbsp;&nbsp; out there. There are still some interesting&nbsp; synergies and fun instant speed plays to be had&nbsp;&nbsp; piloting a Gavi deck.

[6:36] I don’t hate it because it’s&nbsp; a uniquely bad design—it’s not—I hate it because&nbsp;&nbsp; it’s a total betrayal of one of my favorite&nbsp; mechanics. And this isn’t just about Gavi,&nbsp;&nbsp; either. Wizards has a habit of printing what&nbsp; I’ll call “designer commanders,” which I here&nbsp;&nbsp; use to mean commanders that take a particular&nbsp; mechanic and fully dedicate themselves to it,&nbsp;&nbsp; dictating a very specific and often very&nbsp; boring type of deck based around that mechanic. Another example is Eligeth, Crossroads&nbsp; Augur—taking scry, a neat top of library&nbsp;&nbsp; manipulation mechanic, and literally just&nbsp; replacing it with value in a way that&nbsp;&nbsp; doesn’t synergize with anything related to the&nbsp; original mechanic. Cool. These commanders push&nbsp;&nbsp; players toward less interesting decks, and their&nbsp; specificity causes them to offer a narrow range of&nbsp;&nbsp; possible decks to be built.

[7:26] A popular typographic&nbsp; format for this sort of commander is one with two&nbsp;&nbsp; lines of text, one that says “whenever X happens,&nbsp; Y happens” and another that says “whenever enough&nbsp;&nbsp; Ys happen, a Z happens”. This is a template that&nbsp; builds its own deck—you run cards that do X and Y,&nbsp;&nbsp; and maybe even some Z payoff if you’re feeling&nbsp; nutty. Some people might enjoy this type of deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; and to those people I have to say: that’s great!&nbsp; There are certainly people out there who want&nbsp;&nbsp; these kinds of cards—newer players, people who&nbsp; don’t want to spend too long on deckbuilding,&nbsp;&nbsp; or people who like to make unexpected versions&nbsp; of seemingly straightforward commanders,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I think it’s cool that Wizards of&nbsp; the Coast has given them lots of options&nbsp;&nbsp; to work with. But if you want to try out an&nbsp; alternative form of commander deck building,&nbsp;&nbsp; I’m here to offer you a vision of what could be.&nbsp; For the rest of this video, I’m going to talk&nbsp;&nbsp; about how I took one look at Gavi, and then turned&nbsp; around and built a truly bizarre cycling deck.

[8:19] It started with me absentmindedly cruising through&nbsp; random Onslaught cards looking for deck ideas,&nbsp;&nbsp; as one does, and as expected I saw Astral Slide,&nbsp; but also another enchantment I wasn’t familiar&nbsp;&nbsp; with—Overbooked Cemetery. My hope was to&nbsp;&nbsp; find a commander that could be a flexible value&nbsp; engine when I flickered cards with Astral Slide,&nbsp;&nbsp; and this was my shortlist. [9:05] With my commander selected, I set some healthy&nbsp; ground rules: no Sculpting Steel type combos,&nbsp;&nbsp; and a $100 budget. Sharuum’s nature as a reanimation&nbsp;&nbsp; tool means that a beatdown suite&nbsp; with a number of big flying artifact&nbsp;&nbsp; creatures is likely to be a good bet at&nbsp; hundred-dollar-cycling-deck power levels,&nbsp;&nbsp; and will hopefully offer enough beef to win even&nbsp; when Magister Sphinx isn’t available. [9:50] The card offers so many use cases—it’s&nbsp; a reanimation engine with Sharuum,&nbsp;&nbsp; it can be a value, removal, ramp, or lifegain&nbsp; engine with other artifact creatures,&nbsp;&nbsp; and that’s to say nothing of the built-in&nbsp; protection and disruption utility of the&nbsp;&nbsp; card. Out of these strange&nbsp;&nbsp; cards organically grew a toolbox that expanded as&nbsp; I found new hurdles my deck needed to overcome.&nbsp;&nbsp; Wizardcycling grabs a tutor for slide, a tutor for&nbsp; my top end boys, a recursion spell for my slides,&nbsp;&nbsp; and a stifle effect that’s also an extra&nbsp; cycle for my big turns.

[10:36] A mixture of tutoring,&nbsp;&nbsp; discard outlets, and my recursion spell commander&nbsp; means that my deck’s key pieces generally get into&nbsp;&nbsp; play mana-efficiently. The deck does a great&nbsp; job scratching my itch to play a complex and&nbsp;&nbsp; strategic deck, and it does so at a middling power&nbsp; level that feels good in quite a lot of pools. A key takeaway from this deckbuilding&nbsp; process for me was the strength of flexible,&nbsp;&nbsp; ground up deck construction. By starting with&nbsp; a mechanic and a couple key cards and selecting&nbsp;&nbsp; a commander afterward, my deck ended up with a&nbsp; foundation that includes the commander but doesn’t&nbsp;&nbsp; wholly consist of the commander. It’s built to&nbsp; follow general best practices for cycling decks,&nbsp;&nbsp; and though it does include the occasional stormy&nbsp; element or value engine, each of these pieces are&nbsp;&nbsp; intentional, and most of them are tutorable if&nbsp; I decide that’s what a given situation demands.&nbsp;&nbsp; The most important thing I prefer to avoid is&nbsp; having the entire deck stand on the shoulders of&nbsp;&nbsp; the commander, at least not unless you’re ready&nbsp; for that sort of responsibility.

[11:29] I could have&nbsp;&nbsp; made the commander Zur, but then the issue of not&nbsp; having a consistent value source combined with Zur&nbsp;&nbsp; being a removal magnet would have rapidly turned&nbsp; my deck into one more Zur than Astral Slide,&nbsp;&nbsp; as draw enchantments and protection spells gobble&nbsp; up my deck space. It’s good to keep in mind the&nbsp;&nbsp; sort of deckbuilding incentives a given commander&nbsp; pushes onto you, as even one that seems like a&nbsp;&nbsp; good idea on paper might make your deck make&nbsp; a turn that isn’t what you were looking for. If I was to wrap this up in a bow, I’d go&nbsp; with this: when you’re building a deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; figure out what the deck wants to do, and try to&nbsp; turn that into a somewhat focused plan. Accept&nbsp;&nbsp; that you won’t nail the first version of the&nbsp; deck, and that there’s room to adapt as you&nbsp;&nbsp; encounter further problems.

[12:11] I’d say there&nbsp; are four key versions of any single deck:&nbsp;&nbsp; the first principles version in your brain,&nbsp; the draft version after you actually hammer&nbsp;&nbsp; out a 100 card list, the streamlined version&nbsp; completed after you solo playtest a bit,&nbsp;&nbsp; and the “final” version completed after you&nbsp; play it against other people for a while.&nbsp;&nbsp; Each of these steps will solve a different set of&nbsp; problems that the deck has, and that’s a strength.
