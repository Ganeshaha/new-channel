To start with, let’s talk about a pretty&nbsp; conventional way of constructing a commander deck.&nbsp;&nbsp; You find a commander that looks cool, you find&nbsp; cards that synergize well with said commander,&nbsp;&nbsp; and then you fill in the gaps with the&nbsp; other thing the deck needs to succeed.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is a simple and malleable format,&nbsp; but there are other options out there.&nbsp;&nbsp; To talk about those, a useful exercise is&nbsp; to think about the rules of the commander&nbsp;&nbsp; format as if you’ve never seen them before.&nbsp; You’re building a 99 card singleton deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; something with a naturally high degree of&nbsp; variability, but you have an ace up your sleeve:&nbsp;&nbsp; a card that you will always have, every single&nbsp; game.

[1:04] You could make that card Krenko, Mob Boss&nbsp;&nbsp; and then play a goblins deck. And that’s a&nbsp; perfectly valid plan. But something you might&nbsp;&nbsp; notice about that idea is that the commander and&nbsp; the deck do basically the same thing—play goblins. A lot of strong decks will opt to have the&nbsp; commander do something tangential to the rest of&nbsp;&nbsp; the deck, tying in with some key cards but broadly&nbsp; offering something different in nature than the&nbsp;&nbsp; rest of the deck. However, my favorite form of&nbsp; this relationship is where the commander does&nbsp;&nbsp; the exact opposite of the thing that the rest of&nbsp; the deck is trying to do. Basically you pick out&nbsp;&nbsp; a commander, and then you build a midrange-y deck&nbsp; with a glaring weakness solved by that commander.&nbsp;&nbsp; An example that comes to mind is a Glissa&nbsp; Sunslayer deck that I built several months back.

[2:02] The card is a fantastic creature for&nbsp; three mana—an absurdly effective blocker,&nbsp;&nbsp; a draw engine, and a source of endless&nbsp; sadness for decks running enchantments.&nbsp;&nbsp; The EDHRec page seems to consist mostly of decks&nbsp; trying to capitalize on removing counters from&nbsp;&nbsp; your cards, but I instead opted to build a&nbsp; hardcore battlecruiser deck. The commander’s&nbsp;&nbsp; flexibility and daunting presence means that I can&nbsp; fully forget about early game creatures and not&nbsp;&nbsp; worry as much about draw, instead jamming my deck&nbsp; to the brim with ramp, removal, and massive cards.&nbsp;&nbsp; I have a top-notch blocker that’s also a draw&nbsp; engine every single game, and that lets me use&nbsp;&nbsp; my deck slots to focus on being the greedy&nbsp; bitch that I truly want to be deep inside.

[2:50] Another example of this is a&nbsp; Liesa deck that I built recently.&nbsp;&nbsp; When I first saw Liesa, I went “god, that card&nbsp; could absolutely beat the tar out of people.&nbsp;&nbsp; All I want in this world is to mercilessly&nbsp; beat my opponents with that angel.”&nbsp;&nbsp; So great, my commander is selected. How to build a&nbsp; deck around it? Well, judging by the EDHRec page,&nbsp;&nbsp; most people’s answer to that question&nbsp; is to put it in a lifegain deck. Now,&nbsp;&nbsp; I don’t hate this approach. Y’know, run a bunch&nbsp; of cards that gain life and some lifegain synergy,&nbsp;&nbsp; and a commander that will sap life out of both you&nbsp; and your opponents. It makes sense, and you could&nbsp;&nbsp; even argue it sort of fits into my template&nbsp; here.

[3:31] But that’s not the direction I went. Instead, I decided to build a deck that’s just&nbsp; dying, craving for a beater to cut through a stale&nbsp;&nbsp; boardstate—I built a hatebears deck. I crammed as&nbsp; many hate bears as I possibly could into the deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; just truly an upsetting wall of little asshole&nbsp; guys trying to ruin the day of my poor opponents.&nbsp;&nbsp; And the deck has ended up working exactly&nbsp; as intended in a good portion of games—the&nbsp;&nbsp; cards in the deck chip in damage, prevent damage&nbsp; coming at me, and generally hinder my opponents in&nbsp;&nbsp; all sorts of ways, and then Liesa cuts through the&nbsp; resulting board quagmire to reduce my opponents to&nbsp;&nbsp; a paste-like homogenate.

[4:19] By design, it’s&nbsp; a deck with roughly a Liesa-shaped hole,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I’m able to play Liesa for 5 mana&nbsp; anytime I want in any game I want. There’s a lot of room for variation here, but this&nbsp; gives a decent idea of the basic formula. Running&nbsp;&nbsp; a commander that covers a particular element of&nbsp; the deck allows a player to build a deck that puts&nbsp;&nbsp; that element on the backburner, instead focusing&nbsp; all its effort on the things that it knows it can&nbsp;&nbsp; succeed at. This way, you can effectively&nbsp; just solve the problem you know your deck&nbsp;&nbsp; will have ahead of time instead of agonizing&nbsp; over how many deck slots to devote to it.&nbsp;&nbsp; I certainly don’t like the prospect of figuring&nbsp; out how to kill my opponents consistently with&nbsp;&nbsp; a low to mid-budget hatebears deck,&nbsp; so I just avoided that question.&nbsp;&nbsp; Midrange is often something that people&nbsp; look down on for being boring or bland,&nbsp;&nbsp; but with some creative usage of the rules of&nbsp; the format you can build a really fun deck&nbsp;&nbsp; that exists within the midrange realm, and&nbsp; I think more people should try doing that.
