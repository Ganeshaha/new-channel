[0:00] I think most of us have seen games like this: it’s&nbsp; turn five or so, and a player is getting ready to&nbsp;&nbsp; go to attacks. Immediately, a chorus erupts from&nbsp; the would-be attacker’s opponents. “C’mon man,&nbsp;&nbsp; I don’t have anything!” “Hey don’t attack&nbsp; me, I missed a land drop.” “Really,&nbsp;&nbsp; you’re gonna attack me, my deck is just trying&nbsp; to be funny.” I like to call this style of table&nbsp;&nbsp; engagement SBS—“Smol Bean Syndrome”—when a player&nbsp; goes out of their way to paint their deck as being&nbsp;&nbsp; in a bad spot and deserving of nothing but mercy.&nbsp; Now, commander’s nature as a multiplayer format&nbsp;&nbsp; means that table politics naturally play a&nbsp; major role in a lot of games. In addition,&nbsp;&nbsp; a lot of people play EDH specifically for a less&nbsp; cutthroat MTG experience—not everybody at every&nbsp;&nbsp; table is trying to win quickly, or even win at&nbsp; all.

[0:43] I’m not a big fan of most forms of Smol Bean&nbsp;&nbsp; Syndrome due to their tendency to discourage&nbsp; attacks and artificially lengthen games,&nbsp;&nbsp; but I can accept that not everybody is&nbsp; trying to play the same kind of EDH I am. However, there are also people who share my&nbsp; desire to play quicker and more cutthroat games,&nbsp;&nbsp; and who employ Smol Bean Syndrome more&nbsp; tactically. This may be intentional,&nbsp;&nbsp; but it also might not be, which brings me&nbsp; to a major reason for SBS’s existence—deck&nbsp;&nbsp; inconsistency. If somebody has an&nbsp; inconsistent deck, one with some&nbsp;&nbsp; powerful and/or explosive cards but a lack of&nbsp; support or connective tissue for such cards,&nbsp;&nbsp; there will naturally be moments of popping off and&nbsp; moments of the opposite of popping off. This means&nbsp;&nbsp; that such a player can be telling the truth&nbsp; when they say they’re having a whiff moment,&nbsp;&nbsp; but this can still be a deception in some sense&nbsp; of the word.

[1:32] Let’s try an exercise real quick. Here’s a graph of their deck’s performance,&nbsp; with the Y-axis being power and the X-axis&nbsp;&nbsp; being percentile of power at a given moment,&nbsp; you know 20% of the time they’ll be at least&nbsp;&nbsp; this level of powerful, 80% of the time they’ll&nbsp; be at least this much lower quantity of powerful.&nbsp;&nbsp; Make sense? No? Great. You’ve obviously got&nbsp; this moderately small percentage of the time&nbsp;&nbsp; where their deck is absolutely going ham,&nbsp; doing whatever it is that powerful decks do,&nbsp;&nbsp; but also this gulf back here where their deck is&nbsp; farting around doing nothing. Certainly, they’re&nbsp;&nbsp; having a bad time here. But this is often a&nbsp; situation arising from deckbuilding choices—a deck&nbsp;&nbsp; that dedicates too much space to the synergistic&nbsp; and the explosive will have some big turns and&nbsp;&nbsp; occasionally massive wins, but also a lot of&nbsp; moments where it withers to a single removal&nbsp;&nbsp; spell, or just can’t quite put the pieces of the&nbsp; puzzle together.

[2:23] These moments could be made less&nbsp;&nbsp; frequent by running more of that connective&nbsp; tissue—more filtering, more small-scale draw,&nbsp;&nbsp; and more small creatures and low-cost interaction&nbsp; cards to protect themself. If they’re ramping&nbsp;&nbsp; to big cards, they could run a larger proportion&nbsp; of small-scale ramp spells in their deck so they&nbsp;&nbsp; successfully ramp to where they want to be more&nbsp; than 40% of the time. When a person is piloting a&nbsp;&nbsp; sort of all-or-nothing deck, Smol Bean Syndrome is&nbsp; a form of power-level smoothing, a way of papering&nbsp;&nbsp; over the power crater they made for themselves and&nbsp; bolstering these weakest moments. And I don’t mean&nbsp;&nbsp; this as an attack necessarily—if this sounds like&nbsp; you, please, take this as deck building advice. A related concept to Smol Bean Syndrome is people&nbsp; who don’t verbally talk down their game state,&nbsp;&nbsp; but who do play in a way that conceals how&nbsp; well they’re doing.

[3:07] Now, at a cEDH table,&nbsp;&nbsp; this is the norm, and as it should be at that&nbsp; power. You’re trying to win a cutthroat game of&nbsp;&nbsp; magic against other people trying to win even more&nbsp; cutthroatedly, and you have every right to bluff,&nbsp;&nbsp; to downplay your game state, and to otherwise&nbsp; engage in low-level deception. At a more&nbsp;&nbsp; middling power level though, this might not&nbsp; be the best way to go. If you’re playing a&nbsp;&nbsp; deck with a clear wincon, people know what to&nbsp; expect even if they’re a bit scared about it,&nbsp;&nbsp; but if you’re playing a deck whose wins&nbsp; result from weird tech pieces interacting&nbsp;&nbsp; to create unexpected avalanches of value&nbsp; and boardstate—well, people might get a&nbsp;&nbsp; bit apprehensive. When a deck is doing a&nbsp; lot of things but it’s unclear how it wins,&nbsp;&nbsp; people may be asking themselves the question&nbsp; “oh god, is it that?

[3:50] Or is it that?” Additionally, there are some strategic ways of&nbsp; playing that might involve saving resources, and&nbsp;&nbsp; trying to force other people to use interaction&nbsp; first. You might say to yourself, “hmm that card&nbsp;&nbsp; is spooky, but if I bluff no removal somebody&nbsp; else might deal with it and they’ll have less&nbsp;&nbsp; removal when I wanna do something spooky later.”&nbsp; My friend Mr. Hans calls this “playing like a&nbsp;&nbsp; nerd,” and though it’s decent from a strategic&nbsp; standpoint, it’s the kind of slightly-ratty&nbsp;&nbsp; playstyle that people will eventually catch onto.&nbsp; Whether or not you get punished for playing shady&nbsp;&nbsp; or playing nerdy will depend on the table, but&nbsp; you should keep in mind that human beings have&nbsp;&nbsp; a natural tendency to fear the unknown, even if&nbsp; being unknown might feel like the correct play.

[4:36] For an example of a deck that performs almost the&nbsp; opposite way, let’s talk about my Glissa deck—I&nbsp;&nbsp; talk about it a lot, and I will do so again here.&nbsp; That deck consists of ramp, draw spells, removal,&nbsp;&nbsp; and big creatures. No bursty wins, no surprises,&nbsp; and no weird tech pieces or out of place synergy&nbsp;&nbsp; cards hogging space. There’s nothing spicy&nbsp; with the commander either—she just adds draw&nbsp;&nbsp; to improve consistency and is a good blocker to&nbsp; help with survivability. The deck simply does its&nbsp;&nbsp; battlecruiser thing—people who’ve played against&nbsp; the deck know that’s what it does, and if people&nbsp;&nbsp; ask me what it does that’s what I’ll tell them.&nbsp; If I’m currently behind while playing that deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; well guess what, when I get back on my feet&nbsp; you can expect more ramp, draw, big creatures,&nbsp;&nbsp; and removal. Get ready. It’s a very consistent&nbsp; deck, and it’s also what I’ll call a transparent&nbsp;&nbsp; deck.

[5:22] By this I mean, you can always approximate&nbsp; how well it’s doing by looking at the boardstate,&nbsp;&nbsp; cards in hand, and land count of the&nbsp; deck. If the deck has a big board,&nbsp;&nbsp; well the threat is right there, no extreme&nbsp; synergies or combos to worry about later.&nbsp;&nbsp; If the deck has a lot of cards in hand, there’s a&nbsp; good chance it’s packing a lot of removal. If the&nbsp;&nbsp; deck has a big land count, there are all sorts of&nbsp; moderately scary things you need to worry about,&nbsp;&nbsp; but the odds of the spookiest outcomes are&nbsp; lessened the fewer cards in hand the deck&nbsp;&nbsp; has. It’s a powerful deck against a lot of&nbsp; conventionally built mid-power decks—I call&nbsp;&nbsp; it Midrange-Crusher 5000 for a reason—but&nbsp; the transparency and absence of sudden&nbsp;&nbsp; wincons means that people tend to worry&nbsp; about it less than they probably should. Another transparent deck I have is my Meria&nbsp; deck, and though that one has more unique tech&nbsp;&nbsp; and response plays, it’s transparent because&nbsp; it’s a Voltron deck—the problem is right there&nbsp;&nbsp; on the board in front of you.

[6:09] If there’s&nbsp; a Meria on board with a lot of equipments,&nbsp;&nbsp; I’m doing pretty well. Most of my draws will be&nbsp; exiles off the top, so you’re seeing most of the&nbsp;&nbsp; stuff I’m seeing—the scary stuff is all visible.&nbsp; There might be some protection spells in my hand,&nbsp;&nbsp; but the deck devotes a full 10% of its space to&nbsp; protection, so that much shouldn’t be a shock.&nbsp;&nbsp; Meria also has the advantage over Glissa that&nbsp; it’s low-to-the-ground—my deck can create a value&nbsp;&nbsp; engine with its commander to exile cards, but&nbsp; that value is more small artifacts and creatures,&nbsp;&nbsp; and the deck doesn’t do much if the artifact&nbsp; count is low or the commander gets removed.&nbsp;&nbsp; Even with a high artifact count, the deck still&nbsp; only hits one player a turn and gains maybe&nbsp;&nbsp; four cards worth of value. My $25 Rakdos&nbsp; discard deck is even lower to the ground,&nbsp;&nbsp; only incrementally drawing cards&nbsp; with a handful of scrappy creatures,&nbsp;&nbsp; and honestly that’s the deck’s only saving grace.&nbsp; It deletes people’s hands and makes them upset,&nbsp;&nbsp; but the rest of the deck is just grindy creatures&nbsp; and a commander to clear the way for my small&nbsp;&nbsp; group of 2/2s and 3/3s to attack.

[7:02] People will&nbsp; sometimes attack it out of spite for a bit,&nbsp;&nbsp; but the non-discard portion of the deck&nbsp; is so utterly fair-magic that people will&nbsp;&nbsp; usually go “oh, you’re just doing that now.&nbsp; Huh.” And won’t worry about it too much. All three of these decks pair well with a&nbsp; style of table engagement I like to call&nbsp;&nbsp; fight-the-table ideology—they can happily admit&nbsp; to exactly what they plan to do, they can do it&nbsp;&nbsp; fairly consistently, and they are prepared for&nbsp; the consequences if they become an enemy. Me and&nbsp;&nbsp; Mr. Hans originally coined this phrase to describe&nbsp; the optimal playstyle for mixed power level games,&nbsp;&nbsp; or alternatively games where one player gets&nbsp; a decisive advantage early. If you’re playing&nbsp;&nbsp; more powerfully than other people, you could&nbsp; play close to the chest before popping off&nbsp;&nbsp; with a power-turn and deleting the table, but&nbsp; that’s not really fun for anybody.

[7:46] However,&nbsp;&nbsp; give your opponents the information they need to&nbsp; know and be open about the general shape of what&nbsp;&nbsp; your deck is doing, specifically how it wins&nbsp; and what cards you’re playing are doing there,&nbsp;&nbsp; and suddenly you’ve got a dynamic game&nbsp; where your opponents are strategizing&nbsp;&nbsp; about how to keep you in check while&nbsp; managing their own individual plans. Any deck can partake in this ideology in&nbsp; a game playing against lower power decks,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I encourage giving it a try, but as&nbsp; a method it also works as a more general&nbsp;&nbsp; style of deckbuilding and play. Now, I can&nbsp; already hear some of you say “but Alex,&nbsp;&nbsp; why would I want to be the enemy? I don’t&nbsp; want my life total to be yeeted into the&nbsp;&nbsp; sun by a bunch of quivering nerds who I’ve been&nbsp; smugly informing of their impending demise at&nbsp;&nbsp; the hands of my deck.” Well first, if you are&nbsp; in a dominant position, doing literally that&nbsp;&nbsp; can lead to some really fun gameplay as long as&nbsp; you’re not an ass about it.

[8:34] But more broadly,&nbsp;&nbsp; this method can actually save your deck&nbsp; from some hate, and let’s talk about that. This might seem odd for hate-dodging, but step&nbsp; one of fight-the-table ideology is that you should&nbsp;&nbsp; pose some sort of clear threat to the table with&nbsp; a good degree of consistency. This could be early&nbsp;&nbsp; damage, big midgame beats, boardwipes, stax, and&nbsp; so forth. This will mostly come down to building&nbsp;&nbsp; a deck that’s focused and isn’t filled with&nbsp; unnecessary tech pieces or creatures out of&nbsp;&nbsp; sync with the gameplan, and you can accomplish&nbsp; this step on a shockingly low budget. The point&nbsp;&nbsp; of this step is that what you’re doing needs&nbsp; to be visible. To go back to an earlier point,&nbsp;&nbsp; I’ve seen hyper-techy midrange decks which fill&nbsp; their board with unusual permanents with no clear&nbsp;&nbsp; sense of how they’ll be used.

[9:16] If you do this,&nbsp; people might feel a looming sense of threat,&nbsp;&nbsp; but with no obvious sense of what to worry about&nbsp; this might simply result in them attacking you. By&nbsp;&nbsp; contrast, if the threat is visible, people&nbsp; can make their own educated calculations,&nbsp;&nbsp; and that calculation might just be “eh,&nbsp; it’ll probably be fine.” Similarly,&nbsp;&nbsp; consistency is important because that will be&nbsp; baked into people’s calculations as well. If&nbsp;&nbsp; we take the inconsistent, explosive deck I was&nbsp; talking about earlier and average out the power,&nbsp;&nbsp; it might very well be lower than a deck engaging&nbsp; in fight-the-table ideology. But people aren’t&nbsp;&nbsp; gonna be thinking about averages, they’re gonna be&nbsp; thinking about the fact that you swung for 90 at&nbsp;&nbsp; them on turn 5 last game, and that’s a lot scarier&nbsp; than a deck that consistently swings for 15.

[10:00] Step two of fight-the-table ideology is to be open&nbsp; about the threat your deck poses to the table.&nbsp;&nbsp; This doesn’t mean you need to overhype yourself&nbsp; or give people constant updates on your draws,&nbsp;&nbsp; but be straightforward with people. Verbally, this&nbsp; could mean saying, “yeah my deck generates a lot&nbsp;&nbsp; of artifact tokens, which I can use to accomplish&nbsp; various effects. I don’t have very many right now,&nbsp;&nbsp; so I’ll need to get a lot more and have an&nbsp; outlet for them before I can do anything too&nbsp;&nbsp; scary.” In terms of play, being open can&nbsp; just mean having a thing you’re clearly&nbsp;&nbsp; doing. Don’t let your opponents imagine what&nbsp; you’re doing with all these fishy plays,&nbsp;&nbsp; have a goal and execute clear steps along the way.&nbsp; One easy example for this is hardcore ramp decks,&nbsp;&nbsp; which work well because everybody knows they’re&nbsp; scary at a lot of mana, but there’s no singular&nbsp;&nbsp; land drop that signals the deck being a problem.&nbsp; It evokes more of a gentle gradient of concern&nbsp;&nbsp; from your opponents rather than the black and&nbsp; white concern of a random spooky permanent,&nbsp;&nbsp; and this can lead to the deck flying under&nbsp; the radar.

[10:54] This is doubly true if you’re not&nbsp;&nbsp; running a singular win card like Torment of&nbsp; Hailfire, but rather a win involving multiple&nbsp;&nbsp; cards used in a complex way, since that&nbsp; can kinda just blend together in people's&nbsp;&nbsp; minds as “yeah the deck does big things when it&nbsp; gets a lot of mana,” which is conceptually less&nbsp;&nbsp; scary than a one shot. This sort of ramp&nbsp; deck is a great example of transparency&nbsp;&nbsp; being unexpectedly effective at avoiding&nbsp; attacks compared to more opaque playstyles. I’ve laid out the basic framework, but&nbsp; a natural question that might arise is,&nbsp;&nbsp; “can my deck be played this way?” I do my best&nbsp; to play decks that are relatively consistent,&nbsp;&nbsp; so a lot of mine do end up following this mold,&nbsp; but even in my case some decks will be trickier&nbsp;&nbsp; to do this with than others.

[11:35] Decks with combos&nbsp; are generally more difficult to do this with,&nbsp;&nbsp; as they’re naturally less transparent due to the&nbsp; level of importance they place on cards in hand,&nbsp;&nbsp; which is by definition a hidden zone. For this&nbsp; reason and others, sometimes a deck isn’t a&nbsp;&nbsp; great candidate for this kind of transparency&nbsp; treatment. However, I think that a good exercise&nbsp;&nbsp; for every deck is thinking about what it would&nbsp; have to do to become a good candidate for this.&nbsp;&nbsp; What happens if you remind your opponents of&nbsp; the strongest wincon of your deck, along with&nbsp;&nbsp; the conditions required to get there, right at&nbsp; the start of every game? What happens if your&nbsp;&nbsp; opponents treat that wincon you mention seriously?&nbsp; If your deck is sometimes a slow midrange deck and&nbsp;&nbsp; other times an explosive combo list, well, that&nbsp; might be something to interrogate a bit more.
