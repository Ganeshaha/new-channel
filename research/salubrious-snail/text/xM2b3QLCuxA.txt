[0:00] Hey folks, right up top—I have some opinions about&nbsp; Monday’s bans, and those opinions obviously didn’t&nbsp;&nbsp; fit into this video because it’s a precon review.&nbsp; I made a separate video that went up on my Patreon&nbsp;&nbsp; that gave some of my thoughts, and I also included&nbsp; a portion of that video at the end of this one,&nbsp;&nbsp; so you can stick around for that if you want&nbsp; to know some of my thoughts. With that said,&nbsp;&nbsp; let’s talk about the preconstructed commander deck&nbsp; from Duskmourn that my patrons picked out for me&nbsp;&nbsp; to review—Endless Punishment. What I’m going to&nbsp; do in this video is talk about what this deck&nbsp;&nbsp; is trying to do, what I like about the deck, and&nbsp; what I’d change about the deck to improve it. This&nbsp;&nbsp; particular deck sees Wizards of the Coast delving&nbsp; into an archetype relatively unexplored in precons&nbsp;&nbsp; of the past—group slug—and this particular deck&nbsp; is pretty powerful showing. This begins with the&nbsp;&nbsp; commander—Valgavoth, Harrower of Souls is a 4 mana&nbsp; 4/4 flier in Rakdos colors with “Ward—Pay 2 life”&nbsp;&nbsp; and “Whenever an opponent loses life for the&nbsp; first time during each of their turns, put a&nbsp;&nbsp; +1/+1 counter on Valgavoth and draw a card.” The&nbsp; first thing I notice about this card is that it’s&nbsp;&nbsp; a strong payoff for a very specific action—causing&nbsp; opponents to lose life on their own turns.

[0:58] There&nbsp;&nbsp; are all manner of vaguely sluggy cards that this&nbsp; can work well with, but there’s one specific type&nbsp;&nbsp; of card that this really goes to town with, and&nbsp; that’s cards that deal damage to opponents on&nbsp;&nbsp; each of their turns. The prototypical example&nbsp; in my mind is Sulfuric Vortex, but there are a&nbsp;&nbsp; number of similar cards in Rakdos colors as well&nbsp; such as Underworld Dreams type cards that deal&nbsp;&nbsp; damage to players when they draw cards. These go&nbsp; so hard with Valgavoth because they allow a player&nbsp;&nbsp; to draw three additional cards per turn cycle, as&nbsp; well as putting three +1/+1 counters on Valgavoth.&nbsp;&nbsp; This will translate into a pretty back-breaking&nbsp; amount of advantage, and since a lot of cards&nbsp;&nbsp; that do this with Valgavoth cost three mana, it’s&nbsp; theoretically quite easy to set this up by turn 4.

[1:44] Going into this deck, I was kind of curious how&nbsp; much of this sort of thing this precon would be&nbsp;&nbsp; packing, and the answer is honestly more than I&nbsp; expected. There are 11 different cards that I’d&nbsp;&nbsp; expect to trigger life loss more often than&nbsp; not on an opponent’s turn, and an additional&nbsp;&nbsp; 15 cards that will at least sometimes trigger&nbsp; life loss on an opposing turn. Beyond these,&nbsp;&nbsp; there are an additional 10 cards that will&nbsp; cause some sort of life loss to opponents&nbsp;&nbsp; at sorcery speed, and it seems like this sort&nbsp; of incremental chip damage is how this deck is&nbsp;&nbsp; looking to close out the game. A small part of&nbsp; this package seems to be a combination of cards&nbsp;&nbsp; that draw opponents additional cards and cards&nbsp; that damage opponents when they draw. This is&nbsp;&nbsp; backed up by a beefy removal suite that packs&nbsp; answers for a wide range of threats. Finally,&nbsp;&nbsp; a small package worth of lifegain is here to keep&nbsp; the deck alive when the heat turns up.

[2:28] Overall,&nbsp;&nbsp; this deck is looking to build advantage with its&nbsp; commander while gradually knocking down opposing&nbsp;&nbsp; life totals through a broad range of different&nbsp; cards that can dish out incremental damage. So that’s the game plan, but how do I like what’s&nbsp; going on here? Overall I like it quite a lot,&nbsp;&nbsp; actually. I knew going in that with the potential&nbsp; power of the deck’s commander, a lot of the&nbsp;&nbsp; quality of the deck was going to come down to how&nbsp; far the deck leverages its commander synergies,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I’m not unhappy with where it’s at. As I&nbsp; mentioned there are 11 cards that will trigger&nbsp;&nbsp; Valgavoth on most opposing turns, which is a&nbsp; decent count. Archidekt’s built-in hypergeometric&nbsp;&nbsp; calculator tells me that this means a 57% chance&nbsp; of having one of these in the deck’s opening hand,&nbsp;&nbsp; and a 78% chance to find one of these in the&nbsp; first five draws of the game.

[3:14] Given that all&nbsp;&nbsp; of these except for Kaervek the Merciless cost&nbsp; five or less mana, this means a high probability&nbsp;&nbsp; of being able to set up a hefty draw engine&nbsp; by turn 5. I do note that the number of these&nbsp;&nbsp; cards at three or less mana is only four, and&nbsp; this makes a lot of sense—these cards combine&nbsp;&nbsp; with the commander to produce a value engine that&nbsp; will run away with games if unanswered, so I can&nbsp;&nbsp; understand why Wizards of the Coast would want to&nbsp; avoid a lot of the lower cost options. Though this&nbsp;&nbsp; precon does include easily the most powerful of&nbsp; these, Kederekt Parasite, which is unexpected but&nbsp;&nbsp; cool—as a group slug enjoyer, I love to see this&nbsp; powerful tech piece printed widely in a precon.

[3:57] Looking at the other 15 Valvagoth enablers,&nbsp; there’s a wide variety of different effects&nbsp;&nbsp; here. Six of these are Blood Artist effects, which&nbsp; make a decent bit of sense here—the deck packs a&nbsp;&nbsp; good bit of removal and it also runs a relatively&nbsp; high creature count of 32. The deck doesn’t have&nbsp;&nbsp; much self-sacrifice, but I expect that it should&nbsp; still manage to get triggers often enough for&nbsp;&nbsp; these to feel useful. The rest are a mixture of&nbsp; different things that will lead to occasional&nbsp;&nbsp; life loss on opposing turns. Overall, these have a&nbsp; good bit of variety, and oddly I feel like that’s&nbsp;&nbsp; a good thing with a commander this potent. A lot&nbsp; of players building Valgavoth are going to default&nbsp;&nbsp; to cramming as many every-turn-pings as possible&nbsp; into one deck, and though this does maximize the&nbsp;&nbsp; possible Valgavoth triggers, it also flattens&nbsp; the play pattern of the deck into one that is&nbsp;&nbsp; predictably brutal for opponents.

[4:41] This will&nbsp; lead to the deck being enemy #1 at all times&nbsp;&nbsp; and not having many cards dedicated to the backup&nbsp; plans that will inevitably be necessary against&nbsp;&nbsp; competent opponents. By contrast, having a more&nbsp; diverse range of Valgavoth enablers means that&nbsp;&nbsp; this deck’s play patterns and relationship with&nbsp; its commander are more textured to opponents,&nbsp;&nbsp; and a lot of these enablers can also serve&nbsp; useful purposes outside of enabling Valgavoth. So, the instant speed ping plan looks pretty good&nbsp; to me. As I mentioned earlier, this deck’s removal&nbsp;&nbsp; suite is likewise pretty good—it has seven pieces&nbsp; of targeted removal, four board wipes, three&nbsp;&nbsp; removal engines, and one Kardur. He’s not exactly&nbsp; removal, but he removes some annoying scary things&nbsp;&nbsp; from my presence by sending them at a different&nbsp; player for a turn, and that’s basically the&nbsp;&nbsp; same thing, right?

[5:25] There’s also Brash Taunter, I&nbsp; guess that’s a removal engine if your opponents’&nbsp;&nbsp; creatures are puny. Beyond removal, the deck also&nbsp; has moderate amounts of ramp and non-commander&nbsp;&nbsp; draw. This is a deck that’s gonna be drawing a&nbsp; lot of cards and wanting to pay a lot of commander&nbsp;&nbsp; tax, so having a healthy collection of mana rocks&nbsp; is absolutely a good thing. Non-commander draw is&nbsp;&nbsp; also good—expecting to have the commander in play&nbsp; at all times is highly optimistic, and fitting&nbsp;&nbsp; in a healthy share of other draw is also good.&nbsp; Finally, I support the choice to have some amount&nbsp;&nbsp; of life total stabilization here as well, which&nbsp; includes a few different sources of life gain.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is a deck that’ll attract attacks, and&nbsp; it’s good to be able to weather a bit of that. Finally, the mana base here looks quite solid,&nbsp; if a little tap land oriented. In the lands alone&nbsp;&nbsp; we see 25 symbols each for red and black, which&nbsp; are fantastic counts.

[6:09] There’s a lovely selection&nbsp;&nbsp; of different dual lands—a tango, a cycler, a&nbsp; checkland, a pain land, a fast land, a reveal&nbsp;&nbsp; land, an Odyssey filter, and most exciting of all,&nbsp; a Graven Cairns, Future Sight art. This is great&nbsp;&nbsp; variety in lands for a two color precon. As far as&nbsp; utility lands go, the deck runs Leechridden Swamp&nbsp;&nbsp; and Shivan Gorge to go with Valgavoth, a Witch’s&nbsp; Clinic to grant lifelink, and Spinerock Knoll as&nbsp;&nbsp; a hideaway land. There are some minor tweaks&nbsp; that could be made to tune up this mana base,&nbsp;&nbsp; and I’ll be doing one of them in my upgrade&nbsp; on this deck, but honestly this manabase is&nbsp;&nbsp; going to play just fine right out of the box. I&nbsp; playtested the deck 20 times to get a feel for it,&nbsp;&nbsp; and in the vast majority of these the deck felt&nbsp; pretty great, curving out relatively smoothly,&nbsp;&nbsp; setting up threats and engines pretty&nbsp; consistently, and seeming generally&nbsp;&nbsp; well-poised to push later into the game.

[6:56] In some&nbsp; ways I find this deck tricky to evaluate because&nbsp;&nbsp; it seems like a strong deck, but also a deck that&nbsp; will be a lightning rod for opposing removal and&nbsp;&nbsp; attacks. However, keeping that in mind gives us&nbsp; a good idea of where to start with upgrading,&nbsp;&nbsp; so with that said let’s get into the&nbsp; things I’d do to try and improve this deck. For the rest of the video I’m going to discuss&nbsp; parts of the deck I don’t like as much,&nbsp;&nbsp; and changes I’d make to fix those parts. In&nbsp; previous precon reviews I’ve subbed out 20 cards,&nbsp;&nbsp; but in this one I’m going to drop that down&nbsp; to 15—this base is just really solid and 20&nbsp;&nbsp; would feel like changing the deck much more than&nbsp; needed. With that in mind, let’s get into some of&nbsp;&nbsp; the cards I’m not as thrilled by in this, starting&nbsp; with basically a whole category of card—Sorcery&nbsp;&nbsp; speed slug cards. These are cards that deal damage&nbsp; to opponents, but usually or always on our turn&nbsp;&nbsp; rather than our opponents’ turns, and which thus&nbsp; can’t trigger Valgavoth.

[7:39] These cards do have a&nbsp;&nbsp; valid place in the deck—obviously Valgavoth&nbsp; won’t always be in play, and cards that slug&nbsp;&nbsp; opponents on our turn might be better than their&nbsp; instant-speed counterparts sometimes. However,&nbsp;&nbsp; I do hold these to a higher bar than instant-speed&nbsp; options, and that is a bar that simply is simply&nbsp;&nbsp; not met by most of these. Enchanter’s Bane&nbsp; is theoretically an enchantment hate option,&nbsp;&nbsp; but in practice it seems undertuned and gives&nbsp; opponents too many ways around being harmed by it.&nbsp;&nbsp; Fear of Burning Alive is a cute potential damage&nbsp; amplifier, but this deck doesn’t have enough ways&nbsp;&nbsp; to enable Delirium, and without Delirium it’s&nbsp; basically just a 6 mana 4/4. Combustible Gearhulk&nbsp;&nbsp; is a likewise fun card, and I love it in my Radha&nbsp; deck whose average mana value is stupid high, but&nbsp;&nbsp; it too costs a lot of mana and this deck doesn’t&nbsp; want the draw enough to warrant its inclusion.

[8:28] Speaking of draw, Stormfist Crusader feeds the&nbsp; whole table cards, which might be okay in a&nbsp;&nbsp; draw hungry deck, but this deck has powerful&nbsp; enough draw in the command zone that giving&nbsp;&nbsp; opponents resources isn’t worth it. I exclude&nbsp; Spiteful Visions for similar reasons—that one&nbsp;&nbsp; actually is a potent Valgavoth enabler, but&nbsp; in a group slug game plan you don’t want to&nbsp;&nbsp; be giving your opponents resources to fight&nbsp; you with, because you will be attracting the&nbsp;&nbsp; ire of the whole table. There’s a reason why my&nbsp; Rakdos group slug deck does exactly the opposite&nbsp;&nbsp; of giving opponents cards. If you think you can&nbsp; bribe opponents to be nice to you with cards,&nbsp;&nbsp; I think you’re underestimating how much your&nbsp; opponents hate seeing their life total spiral&nbsp;&nbsp; downward, especially if it’s in tandem with you&nbsp; drawing cards. A number of other cards I cut&nbsp;&nbsp; because they don’t do quite enough to warrant&nbsp; keeping around.

[9:09] Vial Smasher’s ability doesn’t&nbsp;&nbsp; deal enough damage to make up for its otherwise&nbsp; meager body, Star Athlete commits the cardinal sin&nbsp;&nbsp; of giving opponents choices, and Tectonic Giant&nbsp; is fine but a bit underwhelming here for four&nbsp;&nbsp; mana. Grab the Prize and Light up the Stage are&nbsp; fine as filtering and draw options, respectively,&nbsp;&nbsp; but decidedly unimpressive to me compared&nbsp; to some other options in this sphere. Some&nbsp;&nbsp; other cards are strong but feel out of place&nbsp; in this deck—Braids is a sweet value engine,&nbsp;&nbsp; but this deck doesn’t run many tokens or ETB&nbsp; permanents that would be desirable sacrifice&nbsp;&nbsp; options. Kaervek can be nice if he sticks around,&nbsp; but 7 mana is a bit too much to pay for what he&nbsp;&nbsp; does for this deck. Finally, Barbflare Gremlin&nbsp; is a card with potential, but this deck isn’t&nbsp;&nbsp; set up well to break parity on the mana boost it&nbsp; gives.

[9:53] Pro tip: doubling your opponents’ mana is&nbsp;&nbsp; generally not recommended—Treasonous Ogre is a&nbsp; reasonably popular card, and our gremlin friend&nbsp;&nbsp; here gives a three times better rate for our&nbsp; opponents to translate life for additional mana. What goes in in place of these cards?&nbsp; Well, most of my additions follow one&nbsp;&nbsp; core principle—this deck will make opponents&nbsp; square the hell up. One form of this is that&nbsp;&nbsp; the commander easily forms many disgusting value&nbsp; engines, and as a result will be a high priority&nbsp;&nbsp; target for removal. One way I choose to&nbsp; combat this is protection equipments,&nbsp;&nbsp; specifically Swiftfoot Boots and Champion’s&nbsp; Helm. The other way I choose to combat this&nbsp;&nbsp; is Glimpse the Impossible which works as a&nbsp; potential burst of mana, as well as serving&nbsp;&nbsp; extra purposes of draw and blockers.

[10:38] I actually&nbsp; dislike a couple of the cards I kept in the deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; namely Rakdos Lord of Riots and Seance Board,&nbsp; but these were kept because I expect this deck&nbsp;&nbsp; to do a lot of recasting its commander and these&nbsp; cards are undeniably helpful for that. Moving on,&nbsp;&nbsp; I mentioned squaring up earlier, and aside from&nbsp; removal the way I expect this to manifest is this&nbsp;&nbsp; deck getting attacked heavily. Opponents do not&nbsp; like feeling like they’re on a clock, and cards&nbsp;&nbsp; that do consistent player damage over time will&nbsp; trigger exactly that feeling. To mitigate that,&nbsp;&nbsp; I’m choosing to add some defensive cards to the&nbsp; deck, namely goad and lifegain. Goad also has&nbsp;&nbsp; the side benefit of enabling the deck’s Blood&nbsp; Artist effects, since opponents being in combat&nbsp;&nbsp; with each other is a thing that often results in&nbsp; creatures dying at no cost to the goading player.&nbsp;&nbsp; Vengeful Ancestor is a classic goad-granter that&nbsp; also triggers Valgavoth, and Frenzied Gorespawn&nbsp;&nbsp; is another goad ETB creature with some added&nbsp; utility.

[11:33] For noncreature options, Disrupt Decorum&nbsp;&nbsp; is that lovely effect of goading everything, and&nbsp; Spectacular Showdown is a similar card that can&nbsp;&nbsp; also be used to suddenly kill an opponent with&nbsp; an enlarged Valgavoth. For lifegain, Raphael,&nbsp;&nbsp; Fiendish Savior is a lovely package—lifelink for&nbsp; the commander and blockers in the form of devils,&nbsp;&nbsp; and these devils can even enable Valgavoth&nbsp; after they block. I’m also choosing to add&nbsp;&nbsp; Tainted Bonds, which serves double duty as&nbsp; life total stabilizer and Valgavoth enabler. With defense and protection out of the way, let’s&nbsp; talk about other Valgavoth enablers. I mentioned&nbsp;&nbsp; earlier that a common choice for naive players&nbsp; building Valgavoth will be cramming in as many&nbsp;&nbsp; cards as possible that deal damage to opponents&nbsp; on each turn.

[12:15] While I do think that going all&nbsp;&nbsp; in these would amplify the deck’s weaknesses&nbsp; to removal and opposing aggression, adding in&nbsp;&nbsp; a handful of these to improve consistency still&nbsp; seems useful now that we’ve contained the deck’s&nbsp;&nbsp; weaknesses a bit. And I do mean a handful—as&nbsp; I mentioned earlier this deck is already doing&nbsp;&nbsp; pretty decently for Valgavoth enablers, and I just&nbsp; want to add in a few more low-cost ones. Roiling&nbsp;&nbsp; Vortex is a good enabler for Valgavoth that&nbsp; damages players each turn, and I chose this over&nbsp;&nbsp; Sulfuric Vortex because this deck has a decent&nbsp; bit of lifegain that it’d be bad to incidentally&nbsp;&nbsp; prevent. I’m also adding one of my pet cards—Rug&nbsp; of Smothering—which performs the role of enabling&nbsp;&nbsp; Valgavoth when opponents cast spells, along with&nbsp; the similar Magebane Lizard. For a handful of&nbsp;&nbsp; miscellaneous cards to finish things off, I added&nbsp; the MDFC Hagra Mauling to replace Temple of the&nbsp;&nbsp; False God, and I also added another removal spell&nbsp; I really like in slug decks called Cut.

[13:05] It’s a mid&nbsp;&nbsp; removal spell that can be cast from the graveyard&nbsp; as a decent life loss spell, and I’ve found this&nbsp;&nbsp; combo to be quite sweet—you get cheap removal for&nbsp; something when you need it early and then a burst&nbsp;&nbsp; of life loss later when you’re getting to the&nbsp; stage of finishing off opponents. My final add&nbsp;&nbsp; was a card I just like—Caustic Bronco. It’s a&nbsp; draw engine, it can slug—what’s not to like? So that’s my upgrade—improved enablers, more&nbsp; protection, and some defensive options to keep&nbsp;&nbsp; us alive through some irritated opponents. Even&nbsp; without upgrades this is an impressive deck,&nbsp;&nbsp; and it’s a deck that very clearly positions itself&nbsp; as a table enemy. I like this about it—though the&nbsp;&nbsp; commander has synergies that very much demand&nbsp; removal immediately, which is an ever-common&nbsp;&nbsp; commander dynamic I don’t care for as much, the&nbsp; fact that this is paired with a gameplan that is&nbsp;&nbsp; looking to whittle down life totals quickly&nbsp; is pretty fun.

[13:54] I’m also interested to see&nbsp;&nbsp; if Wizards of the Coast diversifies the&nbsp; gameplans of precons—there have been a&nbsp;&nbsp; few different aggressive ones over the past&nbsp; year, and I hope we see more of that.
